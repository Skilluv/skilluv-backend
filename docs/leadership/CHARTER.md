# Skilluv Leadership — charter

Published at `skill-uv.com/leadership/charter`.

> Written in English, like the quality charter and unlike the six that came
> before it. A French translation is in `CHARTER.fr.md`.

---

## 1. Leadership is a trade, and it has artefacts

The usual claim about leadership is that it cannot be evidenced. That it is
about presence, judgement, the room — things you either have or do not, and
which show up on a CV as a title and a headcount.

That claim is convenient for whoever already has the title. It is also false.

Leadership produces documents: roadmaps, decision records, delivery plans,
career ladders, curricula, retrospectives. Each of them is a thing somebody
else has to act on, each can be read, and each can be judged on whether it
survives its own assumptions. That is what this domain records.

Not the title. Not the headcount. The document, and what happened because of
it.

## 2. What we accept as proof

**A document somebody who was not in the room can act on.**

Concretely, one of these:

- A **roadmap**, **delivery plan**, **product spec** or **OKR document** —
  what a period is for, and what it is not for.
- A **decision record** — the alternatives, the trade-off, and what would make
  it the wrong call.
- A **retrospective** — and, three months later, its action items.
- A **playbook** — how a team does a recurring thing, written so they keep
  doing it after its author leaves.
- A **career ladder**, **hiring process** or **team health audit** — a
  structure other people are assessed or grown inside.
- A **curriculum**, and the cohort that ran on it.

What we do not accept: a title, a team size, a list of things that shipped
while somebody happened to be in charge, or a claim about morale.

## 3. Everything here says what it gives up

A plan that pursues everything has decided nothing. A decision record with one
option is a preference. A strategy that claims to cover the whole quarter is
a description of the quarter.

Every artefact in this domain names what it is **not** doing and what that
costs. It is a criterion in every review grid, it is the first thing a reviewer
looks for, and its absence is a refusal rather than a lower mark.

This is not stylistic. The value of a leadership document is entirely in the
choices it closes off; the rest is a status report.

## 4. A claim about people is checkable, or it is refused

This is the domain where unfalsifiable claims are easiest to make. "The team
was happier." "We improved psychological safety." "Morale recovered." None of
those can be disputed, and all of them are common.

So the rule here is stricter than anywhere else on the platform: a claim about
people comes with **what was measured, of how many, and when**. Not downgraded
when it is missing — refused.

That is not scepticism about the work. It is the only thing that makes the
work worth something to somebody reading a stranger's profile.

## 5. Confidentiality is a first-class problem, not a footnote

Almost everything a leader writes is written inside somebody's organisation,
about products that have not launched, systems with known weaknesses, and
people who did not choose to be written about.

Every artefact here declares how much of it can be shown:

- **Public** — it was already publishable.
- **Anonymised** — rewritten so that the organisation, the teams and the people
  in it cannot be identified. **A second person confirms this before anything
  is published.** Not the author.
- **Confidential** — shown to nobody. What is attested is the shape: what kind
  of artefact, at what scale, in what industry.

A confidential artefact counts towards the score and appears on no profile.
That is deliberate: a platform that only counted what could be published would
be a platform where only the unemployed can build a leadership record.

**We do not claim to anonymise anything automatically.** No system can reliably
strip identity from prose — remove the company name and the product name is
still there, the office city, the head count, the three customers in paragraph
four. What we record is that a human read it and said nobody is identifiable.
That is a smaller claim, and it is one we can actually make.

## 6. Written decisions are attested whether or not they passed

An RFC can be excellent and rejected. A proposal can be right and arrive at
the wrong moment.

A domain that only recognised accepted proposals would teach people to propose
what will pass, and the hardest technical writing anybody does is the kind that
loses. So a written decision earns an attestation on being reviewed, and one
that was adopted earns a second.

Both are recorded, and the weights say which commits more.

## 7. A retrospective is not finished when the notes are published

It is finished when the things it decided actually happened, or were
explicitly dropped with a reason.

The attestation rests on seventy per cent of the action items being resolved
within ninety days. Facilitating the hour is easy; the three months of chasing
people afterwards is the part nobody does, and it is the part we count.

Dropping an action counts as resolving it. Deciding not to do something, in
writing, with a reason, is a decision — and a rule that punished it would teach
people to leave items open forever instead.

## 8. Coordination is agreed, not announced

A document that commits five projects is not a plan until those five projects
have said so.

Every commitment a leadership artefact makes on another project is recorded,
and the project's steward can acknowledge it. The acknowledgement is the only
term in this domain's score that **cannot be produced alone at a desk** — it is
the closest thing leadership has to a merged contribution, and it is weighted
as such.

A plan written *about* somebody is not the same as a plan agreed *with* them,
and the schema knows the difference.

## 9. A cohort's outcome travels with its denominator

Graduation rates in this industry are almost always computed over the people
who stayed. That figure improves every time somebody gives up, which makes it
reward exactly the failure it should detect.

Here the denominator is **everybody who joined**. The one exception is somebody
who left because they found work — that is the outcome the cohort existed for,
and they are removed rather than counted as a loss.

The reasons people leave are recorded, because four people leaving over the
schedule and four leaving because the curriculum assumed something they did not
have are different facts about the person who designed it, and only the second
is theirs to act on.

## 10. Attribution and co-authorship

Most leadership artefacts have more than one author. Co-authorship is recorded
with credit, and claiming sole authorship of a document a team wrote is the
thing that costs standing here.

Where a mission's terms prevent naming a client, the attestation says what kind
of organisation, at what scale, and what was produced — never who. The skill is
demonstrable without breaching the engagement.

## 11. AI

Declared, and allowed.

Drafting a roadmap, summarising interviews, generating a first pass at a
ladder: normal work. Hiding it is not.

What an assistant cannot do is the part that carries the attestation: it cannot
decide what an organisation is willing to give up, it cannot chase an action
item for three months, and it cannot be the second person who confirms nobody
is identifiable in a document. Those are the parts that make this a trade.

## 12. Six trades, one question

`lead-product`, `lead-tech`, `lead-project`, `lead-people`, `lead-community`,
`lead-mentor`.

Review rights are granted per family and reach no other: somebody who can read
a delivery plan cannot necessarily read a curriculum, and routing work to
reviewers who cannot read it is how a queue becomes a place work goes to die.

What holds the domain together is one question, asked of every artefact:
**could somebody who was not in the room act on this, and what does it say it
is giving up?**
